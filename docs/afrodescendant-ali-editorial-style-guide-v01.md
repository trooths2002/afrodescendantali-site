# Afrodescendant Ali Editorial Style Guide v0.1

Date: 2026-10-28
Project: PM-009 — AfrodescendantAli.com
Editorial direction: Signal / Pan-African Newsroom
Status: house-style draft; production use remains gated

## The voice

Afrodescendant Ali is:

- clear without being cold;
- intelligent without being academic;
- urgent without being frantic;
- rooted in Black life without flattening Black people into one experience;
- analytical without pretending to know more than the evidence shows;
- connected across regions without treating geography as decoration;
- confident in its questions and disciplined in its claims.

The voice should feel like a trusted editor explaining why a development matters to people who are living through it.

## The central sentence

Every substantial story should answer four questions:

1. What happened?
2. Where and to whom?
3. Why does it matter?
4. What should readers watch next?

A useful internal formula is:

> Fact → people and place → stakes → evidence → next test.

## The house posture

Write from proximity, not spectacle.

Name the country, city, institution, community or corridor. Do not use “Africa,” “the Caribbean,” “the diaspora” or “the Black community” as a substitute for specificity.

Treat Black communities as agents, not only as victims, symbols, markets or audiences.

Connect regions when the evidence supports the connection: migration, trade, climate, culture, policy, family, language, capital or history.

Keep the reader close to the facts and close to the people affected by them.

## Rhythm and sentence style

Prefer:

- short to medium sentences;
- active verbs;
- concrete nouns;
- one idea per paragraph;
- varied rhythm;
- a strong first paragraph;
- a quiet, precise final line.

Avoid:

- inflated institutional language;
- repetitive “historic” and “groundbreaking” claims;
- stacked adjectives;
- unexplained acronyms;
- long throat-clearing introductions;
- moral certainty unsupported by the record;
- language that sounds like a press release.

Use a longer sentence when it clarifies a relationship between places, institutions or timelines. Break it when it begins carrying too many claims.

## The Afrodescendant Ali lede

The opening should usually do three things in two or three sentences:

1. State the development.
2. Locate the people and place.
3. Establish the central question or consequence.

Example:

> A new food-security initiative is moving from announcement to implementation across several East African markets. The immediate question is not whether the plan sounds ambitious, but whether farmers, local governments and young workers will see measurable gains.

## Article structures

### News brief

- What happened
- Who announced or confirmed it
- Where and when
- What is known
- What remains unclear
- Direct source link

### Analysis

- The development
- The structural context
- The evidence
- Competing interpretations
- The consequence for specific communities
- The next test

### Explainer

- The plain-language answer
- Key terms
- Timeline
- Who is affected
- What changes next
- Sources and unresolved questions

### Timeline

Use exact dates, locations and events. Do not turn a sequence into a causal claim unless the evidence establishes causation.

### Case file

Separate:

- confirmed facts;
- allegations;
- filings;
- official statements;
- rulings;
- unresolved questions.

Never write “guilty,” “fraud,” “murder,” “cover-up” or similar high-stakes terms unless the authoritative record supports that exact classification.

### Culture and community

Lead with the work, person or community—not with exoticism, poverty or outsider discovery. Explain the local meaning before connecting it to a global trend.

## Attribution language

Preferred:

- According to [source], ...
- [Institution] announced ...
- Court records show ...
- The report found ...
- Residents described ...
- The available record does not establish ...
- The evidence currently supports ...
- This is an inference from ...
- Afrodescendant Ali could not independently verify ...

Avoid:

- “It proves ...” unless proof is genuinely established.
- “Africa says ...”
- “The Black community believes ...”
- “Everyone is talking about ...”
- “This changes everything.”
- “Shocking,” “explosive,” “brutal,” or “historic” without precise support.

## Headline style

Headlines should be specific, active and useful.

Preferred patterns:

- [Actor] takes [action] as [specific stakes] rise
- What [development] means for [people/place]
- The next test for [policy or movement]
- [Community] is building [specific response]
- What is known about [case] — and what remains unresolved
- Across [places], [shared development] is changing [specific issue]

Avoid clickbait, vague uplift, and headlines that make a whole region responsible for one event.

Do not use a headline to state an allegation as fact.

## Regional language

Use the six regional labels consistently:

- Africa
- Black America
- Caribbean
- Afro-Latin America
- Afro-Europe
- Indian Ocean & global corridors

Use “Black World” to describe the connected editorial field, not as a claim that every community shares the same politics, history or interests.

When a story crosses regions, explain the connection:

- movement of people;
- movement of money;
- trade or supply chains;
- climate or disaster;
- language and culture;
- policy transfer;
- family and community networks;
- historical relationship.

## Originality and source boundaries

RSS is a discovery signal, not a writing source to be paraphrased mechanically.

Afrodescendant Ali may:

- investigate the original source;
- verify facts independently;
- write original headlines, ledes and body text;
- add context from additional sources;
- identify patterns across regions;
- publish analysis and interpretation clearly labeled as such.

Afrodescendant Ali must not:

- copy source paragraphs;
- rewrite a source sentence-by-sentence;
- reuse source images, video or audio without clearance;
- translate distinctive source expression without permission;
- present a source allegation as a verified fact;
- hide the original source link.

## The “Signal” editing pass

Before approval, ask:

1. Is the first sentence clear?
2. Is the geography specific?
3. Are the people affected visible?
4. Does every material claim have a source?
5. Is fact separated from interpretation?
6. Is uncertainty stated?
7. Does the article explain why the reader should care?
8. Is the next development clear?
9. Does the piece sound like Afrodescendant Ali rather than a wire rewrite?
10. Would the headline still be accurate if the reader only saw that line?

## House examples

### Weak

> Africa is finally waking up to the climate crisis, and leaders are taking bold action that could transform the continent.

### Afrodescendant Ali

> Leaders from several East African countries are tying climate finance to food production and youth employment. The promise is significant; the test will be whether the money reaches farmers and local institutions rather than stopping at the announcement.

### Weak

> The Caribbean is in crisis after a shocking new development.

### Afrodescendant Ali

> A new regional funding dispute is testing how Caribbean governments share the cost of disaster recovery. The immediate pressure is financial, but the deeper question is who gets protected first when resources are limited.

## Editorial identity in one paragraph

Afrodescendant Ali reports the Black World as a connected field of people, places, movements and decisions. It is direct about what happened, precise about where it happened, honest about what remains unknown, and interested in the forces connecting a local event to a wider regional or global consequence. Its tone is serious but alive, critical but not cynical, and always accountable to the people whose lives are being described.

This guide governs drafting and editing only. It does not authorize source reuse, automatic writing, publication, recurrence, production deployment or indexability.
