Africa–Caribbean fisheries cooperation gains a new framework

A new memorandum connects two intergovernmental fisheries bodies around sustainable production, scientific exchange, digital reporting, and more resilient coastal livelihoods.

5 November 2026Afrodescendant Ali News Desk4 min read

What happened

On 3 November in Rabat, Morocco, the Caribbean Regional Fisheries Mechanism (CRFM) and the African Organization for Fisheries and Aquaculture (ATLAFCO/COMHAFAT) signed a memorandum of understanding on cooperation in fisheries, aquaculture, and ocean governance.

The agreement links institutions working across the Caribbean and Africa. Its stated purpose is to exchange knowledge, expertise, technology, and institutional experience—not to announce a completed program or immediate funding commitment.

What the framework covers

The release identifies several areas for cooperation:

  • sustainable fisheries management and monitoring of illegal, unreported, and unregulated fishing;
  • aquaculture and mariculture;
  • digital tools, statistics, e-reporting, and scientific collaboration;
  • traceability, food safety, and market access;
  • climate resilience, the Blue Economy, ocean governance, biodiversity, and sargassum management.

It also points to training, exchanges, study visits, fellowships, and joint scientific work as possible ways to turn the framework into practice.

What it does not establish

The memorandum is described as non-binding. It does not itself set a budget, create a fisheries quota, guarantee a project, or establish an automatic investment pipeline. The source says financial obligations would require separate arrangements and approvals, and that the initial three-year term depends on signature and internal approvals.

Why it matters

The significance is institutional: a Caribbean body and an African fisheries organization now have a formal channel for working on shared coastal pressures and capabilities. The test will be whether the promised exchanges produce usable data, stronger monitoring, better traceability, more resilient production, and practical gains for fishers, fish farmers, processors, vendors, and coastal communities.

What we are watching

The next evidence should be concrete: named focal points, an annual or multi-year work program, responsibilities and timelines, joint training or scientific activity, and any project or investment proposal that receives its own approval. Until those records appear, this is a cooperation framework—not proof of implementation.
